Fine-tuning a quadruped’s walking on sand, inside a learned model of the sand

A Unitree Go2 walks on CRM granular terrain in Chrono. Fine-tuning its policy with PPO directly in full SPH, at the sample count used here, would take about 121 MI210-days per run, so we learn a reduced model of this robot’s walking from 5.7 hours of its own recorded data, fine-tune the existing policy inside that model with a thousand rollouts at a time, and verify every claim back in the full simulator. The rule this study runs on

In-model gain is not a result. Every number on this page that describes a policy was measured in full Chrono SPH, paired episode by episode against the base policy, on one machine, with the arm and the base scored by the same binary. The fine-tune’s own reward curve is diagnostic only: it has pointed the wrong way more than once.

What this is, and what it is not

It is a local improvement of one existing policy: one round of offline model-based RL. PPO runs inside a surrogate fitted to the base policy’s own walking, every branch starts from a state that policy actually visited, and nothing is re-collected with the fine-tuned policy. The evidence that it stays local: the weights move by 6.3, about 5.5% of their norm; in Chrono the fine-tuned policies spend 0.0–0.1% of their time outside the corpus, even at iteration 3000; the failures whose coverage was measured coincide with leaving it (seed 6 at iteration 1500 had half its states outside, analytic fine-tuning at weight budget 4 had 16%); and push robustness is bounded by what the corpus contains.

It is not a replacement for the simulator, which produced the training data and scores every result, and the surrogate is accurate only near the corpus. Nor is it a claim that fine-tuning in Chrono itself would give the same improvement: that was never run, because at this sample count it is out of reach (see Cost and provenance).

02

The loop

Chrono’s CRM terrain is a coupled SPH fluid-solid problem: the feet and calves are boundary-coupled to a bed of particles, the physics steps at 0.5 ms, and one 15 second episode costs about 1.4 minutes on an MI210. PPO at the scale used here needs about two million simulated robot-seconds per fine-tune, which is out of reach in CRM. The surrogate makes that sample count affordable, and the verification step exists because the surrogate will lie if you let it.

Doubling the corpus changed nothing

The obvious worry is that the method lives or dies on how much data the surrogate saw. So we more than doubled the training data (920 to 2,094 episodes) and left the held-out set exactly as it was.

It made no difference: surrogate accuracy is unchanged (errdist at 2 s 0.469–0.517 against 0.477–0.520), and so is transfer (−47% to −54% forward on the paths, against −53% to −55% for the original corpus).

The constraint is coverage, not size. Twice as much of the same walking buys nothing; what the corpus does not contain (shoves past 140 N) is what the method cannot be asked for.

Less data

The other direction: surrogates trained on a quarter or half of the corpus, two independently trained surrogates each, fine-tuned to iteration 1000 and scored on the same held-out paths.

Rows are captured before the physics step

The first corpus recorded each row one step late, so the state at a control row already carried the kick from the action chosen there. Every fine-tune built on it was void. The manifest now stamps row_capture: pre_step and the fine-tuner refuses any corpus without it.

04

The surrogate, and the horizon it can be trusted over

Accuracy is reported as errdist: planar RMSE over a rollout divided by the recorded trajectory’s maximum displacement from its start over the same horizon (not path length, which a robot walking in a circle would flatter). A model that predicts the robot never moves scores 1.0, so that is the line to beat, and the horizon where a model crosses it is the horizon it is usable to.

One-step training gives a model that is good for about 2 seconds. Teacher forcing never shows it its own mistakes, so errors compound. Fine-tuning that model on its own multi-step rollouts fixes exactly that.

1 s rollout training holds in all ten seeds; 0.5 s training holds in two
Surrogate seed2 s, 0.5 s-trained2 s, 1 s-trained10 s, 0.5 s-trained10 s, 1 s-trained

Ten surrogates, same corpus, same architecture, different initialisation. After 1 s rollout training every one beats the no-motion floor across the full 10 seconds, in a tight band (0.48–0.52 at 2 s). After 0.5 s training, two do, and one blows up to 11.7. That consistency is why the 1 s variant is in the recipe even though the two are indistinguishable at the horizon the policy actually rolls.

Two stages are a schedule, not a requirement. Training with the one-step and rollout losses together from scratch is stable in every seed. With 40,000 steps (about 2.5× the compute of the two stages) it matches them on accuracy (0.49 at 2 s and 0.57 at 10 s, against 0.47 and 0.59) and on transfer (−52.4% and −51.5% forward, the recipe’s mean exactly). With 16,000 steps, the same compute, it is slightly less accurate (0.50 at 2 s) and less reliable in transfer (−51.6, −46.3 and −34.9%). Pre-training one-step first is simply the cheaper way to get there.

The standard size is the right one: smaller loses reliability, larger buys nothing. One of the two small surrogates barely improved forward tracking; its run came apart during training, and it was not the less accurate of the pair. The large surrogate lands in the middle of the standard range with both runs healthy, and its fine-tune costs six times as much, because every PPO step rolls the model 200 times.

Nor does scaling model and data together. A large surrogate trained on the doubled corpus is no more accurate than the standard one on the original: rollout error at 2 s is 0.45–0.49 in every combination (standard or large, 920 or 2,094 training episodes), so it was not fine-tuned. With the channel study below, the pattern is consistent: every surrogate plateaus near 0.47 at 2 s whatever is scaled or added, while Chrono repeats itself to 0.003. What limits the model is its formulation (the 1.28 s context, the architecture, the objective), not its size, its data, or the soil state it is given.

What the reduced model should carry

The 36-D state is the closure of the control interface: everything the policy observes and everything the reward reads, and nothing about the soil. The corpus also logs, per foot, the contact force from the soil and how far the foot has sunk. Four surrogate families add them, trained and fine-tuned with the identical recipe, three seeds each, scored on the same held-out paths against the same base as the ten 36-D surrogates.

The control interface is enough. The weakest, +forces seed 72, is the one the guard stopped at iteration 546. The reduced model does not need to represent the soil’s contact forces or the feet’s sinkage to teach the robot to walk better on it; the effect of the soil is already legible in the robot’s own motion. What the model cannot represent, the disturbance itself, is the one channel this corpus cannot test (push windows are cut from it), and it is the channel the robustness result points at.

06

Branch length: the setting that decides everything

A PPO branch is one rollout inside the surrogate. The original recipe used 0.30 s branches, because that was as far as a one-step surrogate could be trusted. With a rollout-trained surrogate the branch can be 2 s, and the difference is not incremental.

Short branches fail even with a perfect committee

Every 0.30 s configuration we tried (one-step surrogates, rollout-trained surrogates, and a six-member ensemble with and without a disagreement penalty) makes forward tracking worse across held-out paths, while often looking excellent on the straight-line test. The straight test is saturated and cannot see it. Ensembles land inside the range of their own members, so they are not in the recipe either.

07

Why PPO, and not gradients through the model

The surrogate is a neural network, so it is differentiable: a policy can also be fine-tuned by rolling it inside the model and backpropagating the tracking error through the rollout to the policy weights. That analytic route was what worked in the project’s first attempt, and it failed the one time it was tried in this pipeline. A grid in one surrogate (seed 8), scored on 40 held-out paths on one machine, shows why: it works only in a corner that PPO cannot use, and the corner is small.

Budget. Each analytic step follows the surrogate’s exact gradient, so the policy is steered toward whatever the model rewards, including where the model is wrong. Near the base policy that is real improvement; by a weight budget of 4 the robot spends 16% of its time in states the corpus never recorded and loses 13 more paths. PPO never uses the model’s gradient: it tries noisy actions across 1024 rollouts and keeps what scored well on average, which makes a narrow model error much harder to lock onto, and it goes about ten times further safely.

Horizon. The gradient through a 2 s rollout is a product of 200 steps of model derivatives, and for a gait whose feet repeatedly strike and leave the soil that product becomes noise. Rollout training made the surrogate’s predictions accurate for seconds; nothing trains its derivatives. So long branches help PPO, which needs only predictions, and break analytic, which needs derivatives.

This also explains the first attempt. Its one-step surrogates were trustworthy for only ~0.3 s, which is exactly the regime where analytic works and PPO cannot see the consequences of its actions. Rollout training opened the long horizon, and at long horizon the ordering flips.

08

Parallel rollouts: what the model can do and the simulator cannot

Each PPO update averages over some number of branches. In Chrono, 1024 parallel 2 second episodes per update is out of the question; in the surrogate it is a batch dimension. Holding the weight budget and the number of gradient steps fixed, only the number of rollouts per update changes.

Rollouts per updateforwardsidewaysyawrigid-ground forward

Why the recipe says 1024 and not 2048

2048 reads −33.8% forward across three seeds against −30.5% for 1024 across two (−26.4 and −34.5): better on average, but inside the seed spread, at twice the compute per update. Both were measured at the old stop in a 0.5 s rollout-trained surrogate; the comparison has not been repeated at iteration 1000.

There is a second reason to prefer many rollouts, visible only on hard ground: at 64 and 256 rollouts the CRM-tuned policy costs up to 37% of forward tracking when put back on rigid terrain, and at 512 and above no run is significantly worse. Averaging over more of the model’s state space produces a policy that has specialised less.

09

Where to stop, and why it took two different measurements

The fine-tune used to stop when the policy’s weights had moved a fixed distance (dw 4.0, about 430 iterations), a guard inherited from an earlier pipeline against PPO exploiting the surrogate. It had never been tested. So we ran the recipe to 3000 iterations in four surrogates, saved a checkpoint every 500, and scored every one in Chrono on three terrains plus a push test.

Checkpointiterationsdwpaths, forwardpaths, yawrigid, forwardupright at 300 N

The ordinary path evaluation shows the same erosion if you count what it drops: episodes lost to a fall or to walking off the bed run 0, 0, 0 at iterations 500 and 1000 across the three surrogates, then 1, 2, 6 at 1500, 2000 and 2500–3000. The tracking averages hide this, because a failed pair is dropped rather than averaged.

Why robustness erodes

robot_lab trains the base policy with a ±0.5 m/s velocity kick every 10–15 s and randomised friction, masses and actuator gains. The fine-tune has none of that (one surrogate, one soil, a reward of tracking and uprightness only), so whatever the base policy knows that pays off only when disturbed has no gradient protecting it. Putting the kick back into the branches did not help; that experiment is in Pushing the robot.

10

Verified in Chrono, across surrogates

Scoring is paired: the same 40 held-out command schedules, the same spawn positions, the same binary, base and arm. Three of the 40 are skipped identically in every arm because their commanded path does not fit the particle budget. A robot that falls or walks off the bed is a failed episode, and that pair is dropped rather than averaged. Each episode’s error is the mean absolute difference between measured and commanded velocity after its first 0.5 s; the change reported per axis is 100 × mean(arm − base) / mean(base) over the usable pairs, and “clear of zero” means at least two standard errors of the paired difference.

Across ten independently trained surrogates, stopped at iteration 1000: forward error falls 43–57% on the held-out paths (mean 52%), yaw 52–71% (mean 67%) and sideways 9–34% (mean 28%); on straight walking forward error falls 39–68% (mean 62%). Every arm improves every axis clear of zero except seed 6 sideways. Nothing fell: three episodes in two arms walked off the finite bed after 13 s. At the old dw 4.0 stop the same recipe gave a mean of −29% forward, so stopping later nearly doubles the gain in every surrogate.

The two weakest arms are the cases in When it fails: seed 6, the surrogate that collapses if training runs on, and euler seed 2, which the guard stopped at iteration 961.

No regression on rigid ground. On the same 40 paths over hard ground, forward error falls in all ten policies (5–41%, mean 27%) and yaw error 55–74% (mean 71%), with every episode upright. At the old stop rigid forward tracking was neutral on average; at iteration 1000, fine-tuning in a soft-soil model improves hard-ground walking too.

Does the gain need the soil model?

Part of what the fine-tune fixes is a flaw in the base policy itself (yaw drift), which any fine-tune might fix. So the whole pipeline was repeated with a model of rigid ground in place of the soil: the same 1,200 episodes collected on rigid ground (same seeds, commands and pushes), three surrogates trained and fine-tuned with the identical recipe, and every policy scored on both terrains on one machine (sbel) against one base run.

13

Pushing the robot

Tracking is not the only thing a policy can lose. The push test shoves the trunk once per episode while the robot walks: a fixed force for 0.2 s in a fixed body-frame direction, eight directions around the circle, two lattice positions each, identical for every arm. What is scored is the 3 seconds after the force ends.

Upright episodes of 16, by force
Policy120 N180 N240 N300 Ntotal falls

Two measurement traps, both found by looking at the first numbers

Drift is not excursion. Displacement measured sideways of the push direction read about 1 m even for a push straight ahead, because over 3 s the robot walks 1.5 m and this policy yaws while walking. Excursion is now distance from where the command says the robot should be, measured also over the 3 s before the push, so the shove’s own cost is the difference.

A per-episode threshold compares arms against different bars. Recovery was defined as returning inside that episode’s own pre-push error band, and a policy that tracks better has a tighter band, so the better tracker was held to the stricter test and looked slower. A fixed 0.30 m/s bar is now recorded alongside.

Training with disturbances in the surrogate does not fix it. A robot_lab-style ±0.5 m/s kick in 15% or 50% of branches left 300 N robustness unchanged within noise (10–13 upright against 11–12 without) and cost 5–9 points of forward tracking. At 120 and 180 N, where nothing falls, recovery with and without the kick differs by 1–5 points in no consistent direction. The reason is structural: the corpus tops out at 140 N, so the surrogate has never seen a recovery from anything like 300 N, and it represents the recovery from a perturbed state but never the disturbance itself. A surrogate can only teach the robustness its corpus contains.

So the claim is scoped to tracking, with push robustness held at the base policy’s level up to the recipe’s stop. A harder-push corpus was considered and not pursued.

14

When it fails

Of about sixty fine-tunes at the recipe’s data and model size, one came apart on its own: trained 2.5× past the old stop in seed 6’s surrogate, it learned a policy that tumbles. With less data or a smaller surrogate, bad runs become likely: one of four with a reduced corpus (The corpus) and one of two with a smaller model (The surrogate). This is the failure the method has to answer for.

Hard optimisation does not walk the policy out of the data: three thousand iterations, and the real robot still visits 0.0–0.1% of its states outside the corpus, drifting toward the edge (distance ratio 0.81 to 0.91) but not past it. When a run does leave, it leaves suddenly: seed 6 was unremarkable at iteration 1000 and half outside by 1500, so there is no slope to set a threshold on. And what takes the robot out of distribution is disturbance, not the fine-tune: the untouched base policy goes 9.7% outside under a 300 N shove.

Is it the model, the data, or the run?

Not the data: rolled inside every surrogate from identical starts, working policies put 0.0% of their steps outside the covered region.

Partly the model: seed 6’s surrogate makes PPO probe outside the corpus 5–20× as often as seed 8’s under every PPO seed tried, yet two other PPO seeds in it finished fine. The model is a risk factor; the run is the trigger.

Not predicted by accuracy: seed 6 is the most accurate surrogate of the ten (errdist 0.483 at 2 s). Accuracy is measured on the corpus distribution; PPO searches for where the model is wrong, which is off it. What does make a surrogate exploitable is not known; a direct test would perturb held-out states and see which surrogate answers with free acceleration instead of recovery.

The penalty may not be the thing holding it together

The fine-tuner prices distance from the corpus into the reward, and its original comment called that “the difference between PPO working and PPO cheating”. Four runs with the penalty switched off finished healthy at 800 iterations, including in seed 6’s surrogate, and scored −43% to −56% forward, the same band as the penalised runs.

So at these settings the penalty is not load-bearing. It was written for one-step surrogates and 0.30 s branches and may still matter there; the out-of-distribution measurement stays either way, because the guard reads it.

The guard

Every sign of the collapse was in the training log hours before any Chrono scoring. The fine-tuner watches a rolling window of two signals together (how often branches leave the corpus region, and whether reward has fallen off its best), keeps the last healthy checkpoint, and stops when both go bad. Spikes alone are ordinary exploration; a reward dip alone is a hard batch of starts.

Replayed over 60 logged fine-tunes it trips 6 times, every one independently bad in Chrono: the original collapse (caught at iteration 871, keeping a good policy), two old 0.30 s runs scored +30% worse than the base, and three runs deliberately pushed over with a raised learning rate.

The last row matters as much as the trips: that run travelled four times the old weight budget, stayed healthy, and was left alone, where a fixed distance would have stopped it early and still missed the collapse.

Live, it caught one run early. In the ten-surrogate re-score it stopped euler seed 2 at iteration 961 as its branches began leaving the corpus and value loss jumped; the kept policy is still −46% forward in Chrono, 7 points behind the others.

And it misses some. The two bad capacity-study runs degraded in training (reward 4–6× below its best) without tripping it: their share of iterations leaving the corpus peaked at 44% and 58%, under the 60% limit. A looser rule (reward down over 50%, or value loss over 1000) catches both by iteration 240–520, but also fires on runs Chrono scored as good. No single threshold separates the two sets yet.

So the guard is an early warning, not a certificate: a policy counts as good only once it has been scored in Chrono. Choosing the rule against Chrono labels for every logged run is the next step.

15

A Chrono GPU fault, and how it is contained

Five times in about 3,600 CRM episodes, roughly 1 in 720, Chrono died with illegal memory access reported at SphBceManager.cu:543: once in the first collection, twice in 300 N push evaluations, and twice more during an ordinary collection run that carried only the usual 8–140 N pushes. It is not deterministic: a rerun passed both crash points with identical code.

An earlier version of this page tied the fault to the hardest (300 N) shoves; the two collection faults carried only ordinary pushes, so that correlation is refuted.

Line 543 is the error check. The fault is inside the kernel that sums boundary-marker forces on the robot’s feet, whose only out-of-range candidate is an index into arrays sized to the shrinking active-domain particle count. The hashing pass that builds that index returns early without writing when a marker position is non-finite or out of bounds, leaving a stale index behind; the flag meant to catch that case is unreliable in our pinned Chrono and was hardened upstream after our pin.

The containment matters more than the cause. The process cannot recover (thrust throws from a destructor during teardown), so the evaluation writes its partial record and exits first, and the job script resumes the arm. A fault now costs one episode instead of an arm, and a dead arm can no longer be reported as success.

That mechanism is a reading of the code, not a confirmed diagnosis; confirming it (backport the upstream flag fix, add a bounds check that names the marker) would also make it reportable upstream.

What a policy costs end to end. The first verified policy costs roughly 40–110 GPU-hours: the corpus took ~4 h on 24 MI210s (at most ~96 GPU-hours occupied, of which ~29 are the 5.7 h of simulated walking at 5.09 s per simulated second), the surrogate 3.5–4.5, the fine-tune 4.5, and Chrono scoring 1.1 per arm plus the base. The corpus is reused: about sixty fine-tunes ran on this one, so each further fine-tune costs ~4.5 GPU-hours plus its scoring.

Why not fine-tune in Chrono directly. One PPO iteration rolls 1024 branches of 2 s each: about 34 minutes of simulated walking, in 40 gradient steps. A thousand iterations is roughly 24 days of robot time. At 5.09 s per simulated second that is about 2,900 GPU-hours (121 MI210-days) of CRM for one run, and since one CRM instance keeps a GPU about 95% busy, running 1024 branches in parallel would take about 1024 GPUs at once. Chrono also cannot start a branch from a recorded mid-episode state, because the soil state cannot be restored. So the surrogate does not make this experiment faster; it makes a sample count affordable that is out of reach in CRM.

What this comparison is not. It matches simulated experience, not results. How many samples PPO would need directly in CRM to reach the same improvement was never measured, and could be fewer (no model error) or more. Nor is the surrogate’s experience equivalent to Chrono’s: it predicts a 36-D robot state, simulates no soil, and is accurate only near the corpus. No time saving for an equal result is claimed.

Machines: an AMD MI210/MI300X cluster, an A100 node, and three desktops (RTX 3090, RTX 5060 Ti, RX 9070 XT). Every result is paired within one machine and one Chrono build, and the paired analysis refuses records from different binaries: the same policy has read −9.5% on one build and −39.6% on another.

The soil is the cost; the robot and its contact with the soil are not. The foot–soil coupling is 0.5% of GPU time plus a 2% exchange. The multibody solve costs 4.8× more on CRM than on rigid ground only because coupling forces its step down from 2.5 ms to 0.5 ms; per step it costs the same (~0.8 ms), and it runs in the shadow of the soil step.

The surrogate on the same GPU: one robot advances 10 ms in 3.04 ms, 0.30 s per simulated second, 17× faster than CRM (and only 1.2× faster than rigid Chrono, so the surrogate’s advantage is the soil). At the PPO batch of 1024 one step takes 85 ms: 120 robot-seconds per wall second, ~600× the throughput of one Chrono CRM instance. These are the costs of a reduced model, not of a faster simulator: it predicts the robot’s 36-D state only, simulates no soil, and is trustworthy only near the corpus it was fitted to.

What this says about a hybrid, and about NeRD. NeRD (Xu et al., CoRL 2025) learns the solver inside the simulator and keeps collision detection classical; this model is reduced and runs with no simulator in the loop, which is what makes 1024 parallel rollouts possible. If a learned model replaced only the soil and Chrono kept the robot, the multibody thread would become the critical path at ~1.7 s per simulated second: a ~3× ceiling, against 17× for the full reduced model.

Caveat: measured on AMD MI210 with Chrono’s HIP build; the same scene ran at 3.8× real time on an RTX 3090 (COST.md), so the absolute numbers are per machine, the shares much less so.
